Restoring what was here before.

Northwest Indiana was once a mosaic of native prairie, oak savanna, and wetland. Most of it is gone, and what remains is often overrun by invasive species. At Beall Heritage Reserve, we're working to reverse that — without herbicides.

Our Approach

Herbicide-free restoration

We do not use herbicides. That's not a marketing line — it's a foundational commitment. Chemical control of invasive species works in the short term, but it disrupts soil biology, kills non-target plants, and can harm the very insects and pollinators we're trying to protect. Restoring native prairie without chemicals takes longer and requires more hands. We're doing it anyway.

Our current method

  1. Remove the sod
  2. Lay down weed guard and biodegradable paper
  3. Cover with cedar mulch
  4. Let the site rest
  5. Add loamy topsoil
  6. Plant native species at 1-ft centers

Where the method comes from

Our practices are based on the Xerces Society's organic site-preparation protocols, mixed with our own trial and error as we learn what actually works on this land.

Exploring We're also looking into a future partnership to bring FoamStream technology to the Reserve — superheated water and natural foam that kills invasive plants at the root, no chemicals. It's not part of our current process yet; it would be an addition as the Foundation grows.

Living test bed

An 80×20 ft pilot plot on the Reserve documents each phase of herbicide-free site preparation so the methodology can be replicated and shared — with other landowners, and with research partners like Purdue Extension, whom we're actively seeking a relationship with to bring rigorous documentation and peer review to this work.

Most prairie restoration in Indiana still relies on glyphosate and 2,4-D to clear land before replanting — destroying the very biodiversity being restored. Miles of utility corridors, roadsides, and rights-of-way across the state pass through land that could support native prairie and monarch migration corridors. Most of it doesn't, not because it can't, but because no one has prioritized it yet. We think that's worth changing.

Biodiversity & Habitat

Every acre restored is a home returned

To monarchs, fireflies, and the species that belong here. This is our highest-engagement work — and the clearest proof that restoration is working.

Monarchs & pollinators

The monarch doesn't ask much: milkweed to lay its eggs, native flowers for nectar, and land that hasn't been doused in chemicals. As the prairie comes back, so do swallowtails, fritillaries, skippers, and the full range of native pollinators that depend on the same chemical-free habitat.

In development A certified monarch waystation — native milkweed and nectar corridors so migrating monarchs have a place to stop, feed, and breed.

In development A dedicated observation enclosure where visitors can witness the full life cycle up close. This project needs dedicated funding — reach out if you'd like to help make it happen.

Fireflies

Fireflies are the species that made a lot of us fall in love with summer nights in the first place — and healthy, chemical-free habitat is exactly what they need to thrive. Our firefly work anchors two of our most direct community touchpoints:

Native willows & woodland species

Invasive Management

Reed canarygrass & invasive removal

One of the most direct ways to support restoration is to show up and pull plants. We organize volunteer work sessions to remove non-native invasive species by hand — no experience needed, just a willingness to work outside.

What we're removing

  • Reed canarygrass (the primary non-native grass in the active 1.7-acre plot)
  • Garlic mustard, buckthorn, and honeysuckle
  • Other common woodland and prairie invaders

How sessions work

Guided sessions — we show you what to pull and where. Open to adults and youth groups, scheduled seasonally in spring and fall.
